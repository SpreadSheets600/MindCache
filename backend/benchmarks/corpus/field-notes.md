# River survey field notes

## Planning the visit

The survey team visits the river at the same three accessible sites each month. Before departure, the coordinator checks the weather forecast, river level and access restrictions. A high-water warning cancels the visit; no measurement is worth entering a fast current. Each team tells a contact when it expects to return.

Equipment is packed the evening before: sample bottles, a thermometer, a calibrated meter, gloves, labels, pencils and a paper recording sheet. Batteries and a spare probe travel in a dry container. The team carries drinking water and a phone, but records observations on paper in case reception is poor.

## Sampling water

At each site, collect the sample upstream of where anyone is standing. Rinse the collection container with river water unless the laboratory provides a sterile bottle with different instructions. Submerge the bottle opening below the surface and point it upstream, taking care not to stir sediment from the river bed.

Write the site code, date, time and collector's initials on the label before placing the bottle in the cooler. Keep samples chilled and deliver them to the laboratory within the period specified in the sampling protocol. Record any delay and do not claim that a sample was preserved correctly if the cooler warmed up.

## Measuring temperature and conductivity

Place the probe in flowing water away from the bank and wait until the reading stabilizes. Record temperature in degrees Celsius and conductivity in microsiemens per centimetre. Rinse the probe between sites and check its calibration against the supplied standard according to the instrument instructions.

If a reading is outside the meter's range or the display does not settle, note the problem instead of inventing a number. A second measurement can reveal a transcription error, but both readings and the reason for repeating them belong in the field record.

## Observing wildlife

Watch quietly from the bank for five minutes before beginning a count. Note the number of birds and their behaviour without approaching nests or disturbing animals. If identification is uncertain, describe visible markings and record the observation as unidentified rather than guessing the species.

Photographs can help with later identification, but they must not include the exact location of sensitive nesting sites in public reports. The survey team follows local access rules and leaves vegetation, stones and fallen wood where they were found.

## Recording context

Record recent rainfall, visible litter, water colour and any unusual smell. A single observation cannot establish the cause of a change. Compare readings with earlier visits and contact the relevant environmental authority if an acute pollution incident is suspected.

After the visit, transcribe the paper sheets into the shared table and have another team member check the entries against the originals. Mark missing measurements explicitly. Store the paper sheets by date so a later reviewer can trace a number back to its source.

## Equipment care

At the end of the day, clean reusable equipment and let it dry before storage. Follow the meter manufacturer's instructions for probe storage; some sensors must remain moist. Replace damaged gloves and replenish labels before the next survey. Report a broken instrument promptly so a replacement can be arranged.
